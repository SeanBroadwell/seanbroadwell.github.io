01 / Problem

Students have the tools.
They still can’t keep up.

College students are often blindsided by the intensity of coursework and the difficulty of balancing academic deadlines with everyday responsibilities. Despite access to many productivity tools, they consistently fall behind not because they lack motivation. Existing tools just don't help them prioritize.

Who is affected?

College students juggling coursework, part-time jobs, clubs, and personal responsibilities especially those adjusting to university life for the first time.

Why does it matter?

Poor task management leads to procrastination, missed deadlines, and extended time to graduation and that has real academic and financial consequences.

Why do existing tools fail?

Notion requires extensive setup. Google Calendar doesn’t help with priorities. Canvas only shows deadlines. None of these tools tell students what to do next. They just list what needs to be done.

Design question

“How might college students effectively prioritize and track their academic tasks in one place so that they can reduce procrastination and stay on top of their coursework without feeling overwhelmed?”

02 / Evaluation

Two rounds of usability testing.

We tested a low-fidelity paper prototype with two different peer groups to identify consistent pain points before investing in high-fidelity design.

Round 1 Group 17

Tasks given

Walk through onboarding, add tasks across categories, identify today’s top priority, navigate to weekly review.

Worked well: overall flow felt intuitive; daily priority view was well-received
Problem: “user type” setup screen caused confusion users didn’t understand what it meant
Problem: task-adding entry point wasn’t obvious enough from the daily view
Problem: relationship between weekly and daily views was unclear
Round 2 Group 22

Same prototype, different participants

We ran the same tasks with a different group to confirm whether pain points were consistent or user-specific.

Confirmed: same confusion at “user type” not personal preference, a real design flaw
Insight: users wanted the weekly review to fill in automatically, not manually
Quote: “I’d want it to just tell me what to do instead of asking so many questions upfront”
Takeaway: students want the app to do more of the thinking for them
03 / Design Rationale

Every decision had a reason.

Our design choices were driven by feedback, testing, and research not assumptions.

01

Narrowing to academic task management

Our original question covered academics, health, and daily planning. Instructor feedback identified this as too broad. Narrowing to academic task prioritization made every subsequent decision clearer.

02

AI-generated scheduling over manual planning

Both test groups independently said they wanted the app to plan for them. Research on procrastination shows the barrier is often cognitive overload at the planning stage. The AI week plan feature directly addresses this.

03

“Start Here” over a flat task list

A flat list of 20 tasks creates the same paralysis as no list at all. The tiered system (Start Here, Later Today, Optional) means students immediately know what to focus on without having to decide for themselves.

04

Simplifying onboarding based on testing

Both test groups got stuck at the setup screen. We redesigned onboarding to get users to value faster. Google sign-in, enter a name, done. Setup friction was identified as the primary reason students abandon productivity apps.

05

Subtasks to reduce task avoidance

Research on procrastination shows tasks feel more manageable when broken into smaller steps. Each task can be broken into subtasks, so large assignments feel approachable rather than overwhelming.

05 / Reflection

What we learned building this.

The biggest lesson was that narrowing our focus made everything better. Once we committed to academic task prioritization specifically, our design decisions became clearer and more intentional.

Feedback that shaped the design

Our instructor’s early feedback that our design question was too broad forced us to refocus. That one change made everything downstream clearer. Usability testing confirmed both groups hit the same wall at onboarding, which confirmed it was a design problem, not a user issue.

Challenges we faced

Scope creep was our biggest enemy. Every new feature seemed reasonable on its own, but together they made the core experience muddier. Cutting ideas that didn't serve the main problem was honestly harder than building the app.

What testing taught us

Students don’t abandon apps because they lack motivation they quit because the setup is too much work upfront. If someone has to do too much before seeing why it's useful, they're gone. That finding drove both the onboarding redesign and the AI scheduling feature.

What we’d change with more time

We’d properly test the AI scheduling feature specifically whether students trust auto-generated schedules. We’d also run a longitudinal study to measure behavior change over two to four weeks, not just usability in a single session.

06 / Limitations

No design is perfect.

Here's an honest look at what Turtle Life doesn't do yet, and where it could go.

Limitation

AI not yet tested

The AI scheduling feature exists as a concept. We haven’t tested whether students trust auto-generated plans or how they respond when the schedule doesn’t match their preferences.

Limitation

Assumes consistent input

The app only works if students keep their tasks updated. The students who most need help are also least likely to maintain that habit.

Limitation

No mental health layer

Procrastination is sometimes rooted in anxiety or burnout, not just poor planning. Turtle Life doesn’t address the emotional side of student life a future version should.

Future

Smarter AI over time

The next version would learn a student’s patterns and proactively surface the right task at the right moment less of a tool you open and more of a system that quietly keeps you on track.

Future

Group project collaboration

A shared task group feature would let teams stay coordinated without switching to yet another app.

Future

Home screen widget

A lightweight widget showing just the top three tasks would reduce friction even further check what's next without opening the full app.
